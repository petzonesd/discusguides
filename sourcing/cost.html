How Much Does It Cost to Keep Discus?

By Pet Zone SD Team • October 2026

The Essentials
  • Modest 55-gallon startup: roughly $600–900 all-in before fish
  • Full 75–125 gallon adult setup with canister + RO/DI: roughly $1,200–2,000+
  • The fish themselves ($20–40 each for common strains) are the smallest line item
  • Biggest recurring cost is heater electricity, not food
  • Discus cost more to keep than most tropical fish — warmer water, bigger tanks, more frequent changes

Why Discus Cost More Than a Typical Community Tank

Three things drive the budget up compared to most freshwater fish, and all three trace back to what discus actually need rather than to any one expensive piece of gear: the shoaling group size (six or more) forces a genuinely large tank; the 82–86°F target temperature (water chemistry) runs well above standard community-tank heating; and the 25–50% water changes every one to two days that keepers rely on instead of the filter mean more conditioner, more RO/DI consumables if you run one, and more water utility cost than a tank that gets a weekly 20% change.

Startup Costs

These are one-time purchases. Figures below are current retail ranges for standard equipment, not discount or premium-brand pricing — shop sales and used gear can bring real costs down meaningfully.

That lands a modest setup around $600–900 before shipping or tax, and a full adult setup with a real canister filter and an RO/DI unit around $1,200–2,000+, before accounting for any strain priced well above the $20–40 common-strain range covered in the sourcing guide. A used tank and stand, or a secondhand canister filter from a local aquarium club, can meaningfully undercut the low end without compromising the fish's actual needs — the trade-offs worth protecting are the heater's reliability and the tank's structural soundness, not the brand on the box.

Ongoing Monthly Costs

Recurring costs are where discus genuinely separate from most other tropical fish, almost entirely because of temperature and water-change frequency rather than any single expensive consumable.

Electricity

This is usually the single largest recurring line item, and it's easy to underestimate because it's invisible on the shelf. A 300W heater running on a thermostat doesn't run continuously, but a 75–125 gallon tank held at a steady 82–86°F — well above the 76–78°F most community tanks target — draws meaningfully more than a standard setup, and that cost compounds over the one-to-two-year commitment most keepers make. There's no single honest number here since it depends on ambient room temperature, tank size, and local electricity rates; treat any flat "$X/month" claim you see elsewhere with the same skepticism, and expect it to land somewhere between a modest community tank's heating cost and a small space heater's.

Food

A rotation of discus-formulated pellets, frozen beef heart mix, and frozen bloodworms for a group of six to eight runs roughly $25–40 a month for pellets and frozen food combined — frozen beef heart mix alone runs around $7–8 per 3.5 oz pack of cubes, and a active group goes through several packs monthly. See the feeding guide for the full rotation and why variety matters beyond just cost.

Water Changes: Conditioner and RO/DI Consumables

Changing 25–50% of tank volume every one to two days (see water quality & maintenance) uses meaningfully more dechlorinator than a standard weekly-change tank — budget $10–20 a month depending on tank size and conditioner brand. If you're running an RO/DI unit, factor in membrane and cartridge replacement every 6–12 months (roughly $30–60 per replacement set) on top of the water itself, which shows up as a modest bump in your water utility bill rather than a separate line item.

Medication and Incidentals

Budget something for the occasional treatment round — see common diseases — even in a well-run tank; quarantine discipline reduces how often you need it, but doesn't eliminate it entirely.

Where Keepers Underbudget

The two costs new keepers consistently underestimate are electricity (because it's invisible until the bill arrives) and the eventual upgrade tank, once six juveniles outgrow a 55-gallon starter setup within the first year. Planning for the 75+ gallon tank from the start, even if you stock it gradually, is usually cheaper than buying twice.

Keeping Costs Down Without Shortchanging the Fish

  • Buy the tank and stand used from a local aquarium club or classifieds — glass and stands don't wear out the way electronics do, and this is the single biggest lever on startup cost.
  • Oversize the filter once, rather than upgrading twice — a correctly-sized canister bought right the first time costs less over the tank's life than a cheap filter replaced after a bioload outgrows it.
  • Don't cut the heater budget. A failed or inaccurate heater is the one equipment failure that can wipe out a group fast in a tank running this warm — see tank setup for why two smaller heaters often beat one large one.
  • Skip the RO/DI unit if your tap water is reasonably soft already — test your actual water first (see water chemistry) rather than assuming you need one.

Discus Cost FAQ

How much does it cost to set up a discus tank?

Budget roughly $600-900 for a modest 55-gallon starter setup (tank, stand, filter, heaters, basic test kit) and $1,200-2,000+ for a 75-125 gallon adult setup with a canister filter and an RO/DI unit. The fish themselves are a small fraction of the total.

Is discus more expensive to keep than other tropical fish?

Yes, meaningfully so. The warm 82-86°F water raises electricity cost, the frequent large water changes raise water-conditioner and RO/DI-consumable cost, and the recommended group size of six or more adults needs a genuinely large, oversized-filtered tank most other community fish don't require.

Do I need an RO/DI unit to keep discus?

Not for general keeping on most municipal tap water, since modern farm-bred strains tolerate a fairly wide hardness range. It becomes close to necessary for serious breeding, since softer water meaningfully improves fertilization, and for anyone whose tap water runs genuinely hard.

What's the biggest ongoing cost of keeping discus?

Electricity for the heater, by a wide margin, followed by food. Holding 75-125 gallons at 82-86°F year-round costs noticeably more than a standard 76-78°F community tank, and that cost runs every month for the life of the tank.

Can I keep discus on a tight budget?

Yes, with trade-offs: a 55-gallon glass tank, a single oversized canister or sponge filter, one well-chosen heater, and tap water (tested and dechlorinated) rather than RO/DI can bring startup near the low end of the range. Don't cut corners on the heater or the tank's structural quality — those failures are the expensive kind.
