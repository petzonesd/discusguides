Find Discus

By Pet Zone SD Team • September 2026

Discus Quality Varies More Than Most Fish

Discus quality varies enormously between sources — more so than most freshwater fish, because so much of what separates a great discus from a disappointing one (lineage, growth history, feeding, water quality at the source) is invisible unless you know what to look for. Knowing how to evaluate a fish and a seller before you buy is worth as much as any single piece of equipment.

How to Evaluate a Discus Before Buying

  • Body shape: Look for a round, full, evenly-proportioned disc shape. A visibly thin, pinched, or "V"-shaped body from the side is a sign of poor growth history or stunting, which is largely irreversible.
  • Color and pattern: Healthy discus show clean, saturated color appropriate to the strain. Faded, patchy, or unusually dark coloring in a tank that isn't obviously stressed by a recent move is worth asking about.
  • Fins: Check for ragged, clamped, or damaged fins — a sign of either poor water quality at the source or aggressive tankmates.
  • Behavior: Alert, food-motivated, swimming normally within the group. A discus hanging alone, breathing rapidly, or refusing to engage at feeding time is a fish to skip, however good it looks otherwise.
  • Ask about lineage. A seller who can tell you the farm or bloodline behind their stock is giving you a genuinely useful signal about hardiness and expected adult size. Vague "assorted discus" tags usually mean you're buying blind — see bloodline culture for why this distinction matters.

Where to Buy

Specialty Aquarium Stores

The most reliable general-purpose source — staff who keep discus themselves, proper quarantine and acclimation before fish reach the sales floor, and someone to ask follow-up questions of after the sale.

Dedicated Discus Breeders

The best source for known lineage, guaranteed water-quality tolerance specific to a line, and honest answers about a particular strain's quirks. Often the only realistic way to get less common strains or specific show-quality bloodlines.

Red Flags to Avoid

  • Big-box stores with unlabeled "assorted discus" tanks and no staff who can answer basic husbandry questions
  • Visibly pinched, thin, or stunted stock, even if the color looks good
  • A seller who can't tell you feeding or water history, or dodges the question
  • Prices dramatically below the going rate for a given strain — quality discus is not a fish where a bargain is actually a bargain

Buying Online and Shipped Fish

Many dedicated discus breeders sell exclusively online and ship overnight in insulated, oxygenated bags — a legitimate way to access strains or bloodlines that aren't available locally. A reputable shipper packs conservatively for the season (extra insulation and heat packs in cold weather, ice packs in summer), guarantees live arrival, and ships early in the week so a delivery delay doesn't strand the fish over a weekend.

On arrival, float the sealed bag for 15-20 minutes to match temperature, then acclimate gradually by adding small amounts of tank water to the bag over 30-45 minutes before netting the fish into the tank — never pour shipping water into the tank itself. A shipped discus should go straight into an already-cycled quarantine tank, never a brand-new or uncycled one, since it's arriving stressed with no margin for an ammonia spike.

What to Expect to Pay

Quality juvenile discus typically run $20–40 each for common commercial strains like Red Turquoise or Blue Diamond; rarer strains, larger specimens, and premium bloodlines climb well beyond that. Cheap discus from an unreliable source is one of the more expensive mistakes in the hobby once you count the water changes and medication it takes to nurse a bad batch back to health — if they recover at all. Buying Discus FAQ

How much should a discus fish cost?

Quality juvenile discus typically run $20-40 each for common commercial strains like Red Turquoise or Blue Diamond; rarer strains, larger specimens, and premium bloodlines climb well beyond that. A price dramatically below the going rate is a red flag, not a bargain.

How can you tell if a discus is healthy before buying?

Look for a round, full, evenly-proportioned body with no pinched or V-shaped sides; clean, saturated color for the strain; undamaged fins; and alert, food-motivated behavior within the group. A discus hanging alone, breathing rapidly, or refusing to engage at feeding time is a fish to skip.

Should you buy discus from a big-box pet store?

Generally avoid unlabeled "assorted discus" tanks with no staff who can answer basic husbandry questions or speak to lineage. A specialty aquarium store or dedicated discus breeder is a far more reliable source for known feeding and water history.

What questions should you ask before buying a discus?

Ask about the farm or bloodline behind the fish, its current feeding and water parameters, and how long it's been at that location. A seller who dodges these questions or can't answer them is a signal to look elsewhere.
