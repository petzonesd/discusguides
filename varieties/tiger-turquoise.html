Tiger Turquoise Discus

By Pet Zone SD Team • November 2026

The Essentials
  • What it is: A broken/incomplete turquoise striping pattern, within the Turquoise strain family
  • Not: A separate species — "Symphysodon tigrinus" has no taxonomic validity
  • Size: 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) as adults
  • Tank: 55 gallons minimum for a group, tall (20"+ height)
  • pH: 6.0–7.2
  • Temperature: 82–86°F (28–30°C)
  • Hardness: Soft to moderate (2–8 dGH)
  • Difficulty: Intermediate to advanced

Overview

Tiger Turquoise (sometimes just "Tiger Discus") is a patchy, broken-striping pattern within the Turquoise strain family — streaks and barred patches of iridescent blue-green over the body rather than the full, even coverage of a Solid Turquoise or the fine, continuous bars of a Striated Turquoise. It's a real, long-established pattern name in the trade: Jack Wattley Discus, the American breeding operation that originated turquoise discus itself, currently lists both "Tiger Turquoise" and "Albino Tiger Turquoise" among its strains, and Asian farm lines sell fish under the same name. See our Wattley Discus page for the full origin story of the turquoise line this pattern sits within.

Despite the real market demand, the name has a genuine problem attached to it: some sellers and content sites dress it up with a fake scientific-sounding binomial, "Symphysodon tigrinus," as if Tiger Turquoise were its own recognized species. It isn't, and no such species exists.

Clearing Up the "Tigrinus" Myth

The genus Symphysodon has exactly four taxonomically valid names: S. discus (the Heckel discus, covered on our Heckel Discus page), S. aequifasciatus, S. haraldi, and S. tarzoo — the last two clarified by Bleher and colleagues' 2007 molecular and morphological revision of the genus, the standard reference still used today. "Symphysodon tigrinus" doesn't appear anywhere in that literature. It's a pattern-trade name dressed up to sound like a species, not a taxonomic fact — the same inflation problem that shows up whenever a farmed color or pattern strain gets marketed with Latin flourish instead of being described plainly as what it is: a selectively-bred look on top of one of the four real Symphysodon species (almost always S. haraldi or S. aequifasciatus stock, the species underlying the broader Turquoise strain's farm lineage).

Our own glossary flags this same pattern under its Tiger pattern entry — worth a read if you want the short version before diving into a full-length page.

How It Compares to Other Patterns

Tiger Turquoise sits alongside two other recognized patterns within the Turquoise family: Solid (complete, even coverage with little or no striping) and Striated (fine, continuous bars rather than Tiger's broken, patchy ones). It's a different pattern family entirely from Snakeskin's dense, reticulated netting and from Leopard's discrete spotting — all four get compared loosely by buyers because they're all "patterned" rather than solid-color discus, but genetically and visually they're distinct lineages, not points on one spectrum.

Tank Setup & Water

No different from any other discus strain — see the Red Turquoise guide for the full breakdown (55 gallons minimum, tall tank, pH 6.0–7.2, 82–86°F). As with Snakeskin and other pattern strains, even, non-glaring lighting shows the broken striping with the most contrast; a darker substrate or background tends to make the turquoise patches read more vividly than a bright, pale tank does.

Feeding

Standard discus diet — quality pellets as the staple, rotated with beef heart mix, bloodworms, and live foods per the feeding guide. As with the iridescent blue in Blue Turquoise, the pattern itself isn't diet-responsive — it's structural coloration, not a pigment that carotenoid-heavy foods intensify. Consistent nutrition and stable water do more for how vivid the striping looks than any specific ingredient.

Temperament & Compatibility

Standard discus shoaling behavior — peaceful, group-dependent for security, and fully compatible with the usual cardinal tetra / corydoras / rummynose tetra / otocinclus lineup covered in the Red Turquoise guide. No pattern-specific behavioral quirks.

Breeding Notes

Standard pairing, spawning, and parental slime-feeding — see the breeding guide for the full walkthrough. Because Tiger is a broken-pattern expression within the broader Turquoise genetics rather than a fixed, standardized line, fry from a home pairing can show real variation in how much of the body the striping breaks across — from heavily patched to nearly solid — the same unpredictability covered for other pattern-named strains like Snakeskin and Leopard elsewhere on this site.

Common Issues

Being Sold a Fake Species Name

Cause: A listing or care page uses "Symphysodon tigrinus" as if it were a scientifically recognized species, usually to make a farmed pattern sound rarer or more exotic than it is.

Fix: Not a health issue — a buying-information one. It doesn't change anything about the actual fish or how to keep it, but it's worth knowing the name has no scientific basis so you're not misled about what you're buying or paying a "rare species" premium for a farmed color pattern.

Striping Looking Faded or Patchy

Cause: Like most pattern expression in discus, striping definition can vary with lighting, water clarity, and age rather than being fully fixed from a young juvenile.

Fix: Nothing to treat. If pattern intensity matters for your purposes, evaluate adult or near-adult stock, and rule out basic water-quality issues covered in water quality & maintenance before assuming anything is wrong with the fish itself.

Where to Get Quality Fish

Look for crisp, well-defined striping with good contrast between the turquoise and the darker break in the pattern, rather than a muddy or washed-out look. Ignore any "tigrinus" or similar Latin species-name framing in a listing — it's marketing, not taxonomy, and shouldn't affect what you're willing to pay. Pet Zone SD can special-order Tiger Turquoise stock through its usual import channels; ask about current availability. See our sourcing guide for general buying advice.

Cost: Typically $20–40 for quality juveniles, in line with other Turquoise-family strains — the pattern name alone doesn't justify a rare-species premium.

Final Thoughts

Tiger Turquoise is a good pick for a keeper who wants the broken, high-contrast striping look within the Turquoise family without taking on any additional care difficulty — it's the same fish underneath as any other turquoise-line discus. Just buy it as what it actually is: a farmed pattern strain with real history behind it, not a rare wild species with an invented Latin name.

Tiger Turquoise FAQ

Is Tiger Discus a real species — is "Symphysodon tigrinus" valid?

No. The genus Symphysodon has exactly four recognized names: S. discus, S. aequifasciatus, S. haraldi, and S. tarzoo, confirmed by Bleher's 2007 taxonomic revision of the genus. "Symphysodon tigrinus" has no taxonomic standing anywhere in that literature. Tiger (or Tiger Turquoise) is a farmed pattern name within the Turquoise strain, not a species, whatever a Latin-sounding label on a sales page implies.

What does the Tiger pattern actually look like?

Broken, patchy turquoise striping — streaks and barred patches of iridescent blue-green over the body rather than full, continuous coverage. It sits within the Turquoise strain family alongside Solid Turquoise (complete coverage) and Striated Turquoise (fine, even striping), and is a separate pattern from Snakeskin's dense reticulated netting or Leopard's discrete spotting.

Where does Tiger Turquoise discus come from?

It's a pattern variant within the Turquoise strain family that Jack Wattley's original blue-x-green Amazon cross founded in the 1960s. The name is used by both American/German-descended lines and Asian farm lines today — Jack Wattley Discus itself currently lists Tiger Turquoise and Albino Tiger Turquoise among its strains.

Does Tiger Turquoise discus need different care than other discus?

No — standard discus parameters apply across the board: 82-86°F, pH 6.0-7.2, soft to moderate hardness, and the same 55-gallon-minimum tall tank as any other strain. Nothing about the striping pattern changes husbandry.
