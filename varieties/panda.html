Panda Discus

By Pet Zone SD Team • October 2026

The Essentials
  • What it is: A Pigeon Blood derivative defined by reduced black peppering, not a single fixed color
  • Origin: A 1991 spontaneous Thai "pigeon" mutation, crossed into Jack Wattley's Turquoise line around 1992 to create the first Panda
  • Size: 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) as adults
  • Tank: 55 gallons minimum for a group, tall (20"+ height)
  • pH: 6.0–7.2
  • Temperature: 82–86°F (28–30°C)
  • Hardness: Soft to moderate (2–8 dGH)
  • Difficulty: Intermediate to advanced

Overview

"Panda" on a discus label doesn't name one color the way "Blue Diamond" or "Marlboro Red" does. It names a pattern trait: a noticeably cleaner, lower-peppering expression of the Pigeon Blood family, built on a pale-to-white ground that shows far less of the scattered black speckling a standard Pigeon Blood carries. Breeders then layer a base color onto that cleaner canvas — red, blue, gold, or a bold tiger-stripe pattern — which is why "Panda" shows up attached to several different color names across the trade rather than describing one fixed look.

That naming looseness is well documented rather than a sign of confusion on any one seller's part. Aquarium Glaser, a German commercial discus exporter, notes outright that there's no industry-standard naming convention for Pigeon Blood-family patterns, and describes its own "Pidgeon Blood Panda" as a red-turquoise base with unconnected turquoise spotting and blackish fin tips. Jack Wattley Discus in Miami sells a "Tiger Yellow Panda" built on an entirely different look — a bright white body with yellow-gold vertical tiger-striping that's unique on every individual fish. Both are legitimately "Panda" by their own breeders' naming; neither is wrong.

Where the Pattern Comes From

The lineage traces to 1991, when Thai breeder Kitti Phanaitthi found a spontaneous pattern mutation in a batch of ordinary brown discus — the same foundational "pigeon" mutation that gave rise to the pale, red-stippled Pigeon Blood strain covered elsewhere in this library. According to Jack Wattley Discus's own account of its featured strains, Jack Wattley crossed that Thai mutation with his own Turquoise line around 1992 to create the Red Panda — the strain that gave the broader Panda family its name, and a direct extension of the same Wattley breeding program that originated turquoise discus itself.

From that first Red Panda, breeders have bred the low-peppering trait onto other bases to build out a whole informal family: Tiger Blue Panda and Checkerboard Red Panda are both documented as Panda variants by Wattley's own strain notes, and a Marlboro Red Panda variant is described as pushing for higher red intensity and even less peppering than the standard Panda. The common thread across every variant isn't the base color — it's the comparatively clean, reduced-speckling look layered on top of it.

Tank Setup & Water

No different from any other discus in requirements — see the Red Turquoise guide for the full tank size, substrate, and water chemistry breakdown (55 gallons minimum, tall tank, pH 6.0–7.2, 82–86°F). As with its Pigeon Blood parent strain, Panda's pale ground color makes it an unusually sensitive visual indicator of water quality — tannin staining or a lapsed water-change schedule shows up as dulling or yellowing well before it would on a darker or more heavily patterned strain.

Feeding

Standard discus diet applies without modification — a quality discus pellet as the staple, supplemented with beef heart mix, frozen bloodworms, and live foods for conditioning, per the feeding guide. Diet doesn't influence the peppering pattern itself since it's a structural trait rather than a diet-responsive one, the same as with base Pigeon Blood.

Temperament & Compatibility

Identical shoaling behavior to every other discus strain — peaceful with tankmates, with the usual hierarchy-establishing chasing within a young group that settles as the fish mature. The high-contrast look of a well-marked Panda (clean pale ground with bold, unpeppered color patches) reads as a strong visual centerpiece in a mixed-strain display tank.

Breeding Notes

Standard discus pair formation, spawning, and parental slime-feeding apply — see the breeding guide for the full walkthrough. The low-peppering trait inherits with the same variability as the base Pigeon Blood pattern it descends from, so a home spawn from two Panda parents typically produces a mixed brood — some fry with the clean Panda look, others reverting toward standard peppering — rather than a uniform litter. This is the same mechanism that let breeders split the Pigeon Blood family into so many named derivatives in the first place: an unusual fry gets noticed in an otherwise mixed spawn and gets selectively bred forward.

Common Issues

Yellowing or Dulling on the Pale Ground

Cause: As with Pigeon Blood, almost always tannin staining or declining water quality rather than illness, given how visible it is against the clean base color.

Fix: Step up water change frequency and check for tannin sources. Persistent dulling despite clean water is worth investigating via common diseases.

Peppering Creeping Back In With Age

Cause: Some individuals show more black speckling as they mature than they did as juveniles — a normal expression of the trait's variability, not a health problem.

Fix: Nothing to fix. If you're buying specifically for the clean Panda look, evaluate adult or near-adult stock rather than young juveniles whenever possible, since the final peppering level isn't fully set at a young age.

Where to Get Quality Fish

Panda is carried less consistently at general retail than base Pigeon Blood or the mainstream solid-color strains — it shows up more often through specialty discus breeders and importers (Jack Wattley Discus in Miami is a documented direct source for several named Panda variants) than through mass Southeast Asian farm production. That narrower supply also means pricing runs higher than the typical $20–40 common-strain range: Wattley's own Tiger Yellow Panda listings run roughly $90–150 depending on size, reflecting specialty rather than farm-scale sourcing. Final Thoughts

Panda is worth seeking out if you like Pigeon Blood's clean aesthetic but want a sharper, higher-contrast version of it — it keeps exactly like any other farm-strain discus, with no special care requirements beyond standard discus husbandry. Just go in expecting some genuine variation in what you get: because the name describes a pattern trait rather than one fixed color, two fish both correctly labeled "Panda" from two different breeders can look meaningfully different from each other.

Panda Discus FAQ

Is Panda a separate discus strain or a Pigeon Blood variant?

It's a Pigeon Blood derivative, not an independent strain. Panda traces to Jack Wattley crossing a spontaneous 1991 Thai "pigeon" mutation with his own Turquoise line around 1992, and it's defined by noticeably less black peppering than a standard Pigeon Blood rather than by any one fixed color.

Why do different sellers describe Panda discus so differently?

There's no single industry-standard definition of the pattern. Breeders apply "Panda" to a range of base colors built on the same low-peppering pigeon trait — Tiger Yellow Panda, Tiger Blue Panda, Checkerboard Red Panda, and Marlboro Red Panda are all documented variants sold under different breeders' own naming conventions.

Is Panda discus harder to find than regular Pigeon Blood?

Generally yes. It's more often carried by specialty breeders and importers than mass Southeast Asian farm production, which also means it typically sells at a premium over common-strain pricing.

Will two Panda discus breed true?

Not reliably. Like the base Pigeon Blood trait it descends from, the low-peppering pattern has variable inheritance, so a home spawn from two Panda parents typically produces a mixed brood rather than a uniform Panda-patterned litter.
