Symphysodon aequifasciatus / haraldi complex

Wild Blue, Brown & Green Discus

By Pet Zone SD Team • September 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: 5–7 inches (13–18 cm), generally smaller than farm strains
  • Tank: 55–75 gallons+, tall (20"+ height)
  • pH: 5.5–7.0 depending on population of origin
  • Temperature: 82–86°F (28–30°C)
  • Hardness: Soft (0–5 dGH)
  • Temperament: Peaceful, shoaling, shyer than farm strains
  • Difficulty: Advanced

Overview

Every farm strain covered elsewhere on this site — Red Turquoise, Blue Diamond, Marlboro Red, and the rest — ultimately traces back to wild populations in this group, commonly split across Symphysodon aequifasciatus and Symphysodon haraldi depending on the taxonomic treatment you're reading, plus the closely related Symphysodon tarzoo (green discus) recognized by some authorities as a separate species. In the hobby, these are usually just sold by their common color names — Blue Discus, Brown Discus, Green Discus — tied loosely to the river system they were collected from.

Compared to Heckel discus, this complex is somewhat more forgiving of water chemistry and more widely available as wild or semi-wild imports, but it's still a meaningfully different fish from a farm strain: smaller, subtler in color, shyer, and far less tolerant of casual husbandry.

Blue Discus

Fine, iridescent blue striping over a brown-green base, generally associated with populations from the Rio Negro and nearby blackwater systems. The wild ancestor of every "blue" and "turquoise" farm strain in the trade.

Brown Discus

The most common and typically hardiest wild-type color form, with a subdued brown-bronze body and faint vertical banding. Often the easiest wild-type entry point for a keeper moving up from farm strains.

Green Discus

Associated with whitewater river systems rather than blackwater, showing a green-gold cast and, in some populations, red spotting on the flanks — the wild source of pattern traits that eventually became strains like Pigeon Blood.

Tank Setup & Water

Softer, more acidic water than farm strains need, though generally less extreme than true Heckel discus — exact requirements shift with the specific river population, so ask your source about collection origin where possible. A tall 55–75 gallon tank with driftwood, dim lighting, and minimal disturbance suits all three color forms.

Feeding

Wild-caught individuals may need live blackworms or bloodworms initially before transitioning to frozen and prepared foods, similar to Heckel discus. Semi-wild or tank-raised offspring of wild stock are generally easier feeders.

Temperament & Compatibility

Shoaling and peaceful, but shyer than farm strains — a species tank or calm, soft-water community is the safest bet. Cardinal tetras and Corydoras remain solid, low-disturbance tankmates.

Common Issues

As with Heckel discus, wild-caught individuals can be slow to accept food and unusually skittish immediately after import — patience, live food, and a calm, dimly lit tank are the standard remedies for both. Beyond that, this group is subject to the same general discus health issues covered in common diseases, though softer, more acid-sensitive water means chemistry swings hit harder and faster than they would with a farm strain.

Breeding & Sourcing

Specialist breeders do work with wild-type lines specifically to preserve the natural color forms and support sustainable, non-wild-collected supply. As with Heckel discus, buying from a source that can speak to collection origin and captive-breeding status is worth prioritizing over price alone. See our sourcing guide for general buying principles.

Cost: Higher than farm strains, though generally somewhat less than true Heckel discus — expect $50–150+ depending on color form, size, and whether the fish is wild-caught or captive-bred.

Final Thoughts

This group is for the keeper who wants to see where every farm strain actually came from — subtler, smaller, and considerably less forgiving than a Red Turquoise, but genuinely rewarding for a hobbyist who's already mastered the fundamentals. Start with Red Turquoise if you haven't kept discus before; come back to this page once you have.

Wild Discus FAQ

Are wild discus harder to keep than farm strains?

Yes. They're smaller, subtler in color, shyer, and need softer, more acidic water than farm strains, which have been bred for generations toward wider water tolerance and calmer temperaments. Master a farm strain like Red Turquoise first.

What's the difference between Blue, Brown, and Green wild discus?

They're different color forms tied to different river systems. Blue Discus shows fine iridescent striping from blackwater populations; Brown Discus is the hardiest, most subdued form; Green Discus comes from whitewater systems and shows a green-gold cast, sometimes with red flank spotting.

Do all farm-strain discus come from this wild group?

Yes, with one exception. Every farm strain except Heckel discus traces back to the Symphysodon aequifasciatus/haraldi/tarzoo complex covered on this page — decades of selective breeding pushed these wild color forms toward the saturated, patterned strains sold today.
