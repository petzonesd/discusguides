Pigeon Blood Discus

By Pet Zone SD Team • September 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) as adults
  • Tank: 55 gallons minimum for a group, tall (20"+ height)
  • pH: 6.0–7.2
  • Temperature: 82–86°F (28–30°C)
  • Hardness: Soft to moderate (2–8 dGH)
  • Temperament: Peaceful, shoaling
  • Difficulty: Intermediate to advanced

Overview

Pigeon Blood is the most polarizing strain in this list, and it earns that reputation honestly. The base body is pale, almost translucent white or off-white, marked with red eyes and a scatter of red spotting — a look derived from the "pigeon" pattern gene originally developed in a related strain lineage. Keepers either find it strikingly clean and unusual, or too washed-out compared to the saturated color of Red Turquoise and Marlboro Red. There isn't a wrong answer; it's a real matter of taste.

Genetically, the pigeon pattern trait is linked to reduced pattern and pigment expression rather than a health defect — a well-bred Pigeon Blood is exactly as robust as any other farm strain. That said, because the trait has been used as a base for several derivative strains, quality and consistency vary more between breeders than with more established solid or patterned lines. Two of the more notable derivatives: Yellow Pigeon, the same pigeon gene expressed as a vivid yellow rather than pale white, and Red Melon Pigeon Blood Cross, a hybrid layering Melon Red saturation onto the Pigeon Blood base.

Where the Pigeon Pattern Comes From

The pigeon trait originated as a selectively bred pattern mutation that suppresses the discus's normal barring and pigment density, leaving a pale, almost translucent ground color. Because that ground color is essentially a blank canvas, breeders have layered other color genetics on top of it to create an entire family of pigeon-based strains — the pale white-to-grey Pigeon Blood covered here, the vivid Yellow Pigeon (also sold as Yellow Checkerboard), and hybrids like the Red Melon Pigeon Blood Cross, which layers Melon Red saturation onto the same pale base. If you like Pigeon Blood's clean, minimal look but want more color, those two are the natural next strains to look at.

Tank Setup & Water

No different from other discus strains in requirements — see the Red Turquoise guide for the full tank size, substrate, and water chemistry breakdown (55 gallons minimum, tall tank, pH 6.0–7.2, 82–86°F). Where Pigeon Blood does differ is as a diagnostic tool: the pale body makes it an unusually good visual indicator of tank cleanliness. Tannin buildup from driftwood, a lapsed water change schedule, or general water-quality decline shows up as yellowing or dulling against the light base color well before it would be visible on a darker or more patterned strain. Keepers who run a mixed-strain tank often describe their Pigeon Blood as the "canary" that tells them when maintenance is falling behind.

Feeding

Standard discus diet applies without modification — a quality discus pellet as the staple, supplemented with beef heart mix, frozen bloodworms, and live foods for conditioning, per the feeding guide. No special dietary requirements beyond what any discus strain needs, and no food additive meaningfully changes the pigeon pattern itself since it's a structural pigment trait rather than a diet-responsive one.

Temperament & Compatibility

Identical shoaling behavior to other discus — peaceful with tankmates, occasional hierarchy-establishing chasing within the group that settles as the group matures. Mixes well visually and behaviorally with other strains in a display tank; the pale body reads as a strong visual contrast point against darker strains like Blue Diamond or Marlboro Red.

Breeding Notes

Standard discus pair formation, spawning, and parental slime-feeding apply — see the breeding guide for the full walkthrough. Because the pigeon trait's inheritance pattern is more variable and less fixed than solid-color genetics, home-bred fry from a Pigeon Blood pair won't reliably all express the parent pattern; expect a mixed brood with varying pattern density rather than a uniform pigeon-patterned spawn. This variability is part of why pigeon-derived strains have diversified into so many distinct color forms over the years — each new derivative traces back to breeders selecting out an interesting variant from an otherwise mixed spawn.

Common Issues

Yellowing or Dulling

Cause: Almost always tannin staining or declining water quality rather than illness, given how visible it is against the pale ground color.

Fix: Step up water change frequency and check for tannin sources (driftwood, certain substrates). Persistent dulling despite clean water is worth investigating as a stress or health issue via common diseases.

Cloudy or Dull Eyes

Cause: Because Pigeon Blood's red eyes are a visually prominent feature, cloudiness reads as an earlier, clearer health signal on this strain than on darker-eyed fish — often linked to water quality or early bacterial or parasitic issues.

Fix: Treat as an early warning: test water immediately and observe for other symptoms rather than waiting for more obvious signs.

Where to Get Quality Fish

Look for clean, unblemished pale coloring with dense, evenly distributed red stippling, and vivid, clear red eyes — cloudy or dull eyes are a quicker health tell on this strain than on darker-bodied fish. Pet Zone SD stocks Pigeon Blood when available. See our sourcing guide for general buying advice.

Cost: Typically in the same $20–40 range as other common farm strains for quality juveniles, with denser, more evenly stippled specimens commanding a modest premium.

Final Thoughts

Pigeon Blood is a strain worth choosing on aesthetics rather than any care difference — it keeps exactly like any other farm-strain discus, with the added bonus of doubling as an early warning system for water-quality lapses. If the pale, red-flecked look appeals to you more than saturated color, it's a completely sound choice for a first or second discus, and a gateway into the wider family of pigeon-derived strains if you want to explore further.

Pigeon Blood FAQ

Is Pigeon Blood a genetic defect?

No. The pigeon pattern trait reduces pattern and pigment expression, but a well-bred Pigeon Blood is exactly as robust and healthy as any other farm strain — it's a selectively bred cosmetic trait, not a health defect.

What's the difference between Pigeon Blood and Yellow Pigeon discus?

Both share the same underlying pigeon pattern gene. Pigeon Blood expresses it as pale white to pigeon-grey with red stippling; Yellow Pigeon expresses the same gene as a vivid yellow base instead.

Why does my Pigeon Blood discus look dull or yellowed?

The pale body shows tannin buildup, poor water quality, or stress far more visibly than a darker-bodied strain. Check water parameters and water-change frequency first — dulling on this strain is usually a maintenance signal, not illness.

Will Pigeon Blood fry look like their parents?

Not reliably. The pigeon trait's inheritance is more variable than solid-color genetics, so a home-bred spawn from two Pigeon Blood parents will typically produce a mix of pattern expressions rather than a uniform pigeon-patterned brood.
