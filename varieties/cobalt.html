Cobalt Discus

By Pet Zone SD Team • November 2026

The Essentials
  • What it is: A solid, deep metallic-blue strain that still carries visible transverse banding
  • Not: The same strain as Blue Diamond — related, but bred to a different standard
  • Size: 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) as adults
  • Tank: 55 gallons minimum for a group, tall (20"+ height)
  • pH: 6.0–7.2
  • Temperature: 82–86°F (28–30°C)
  • Hardness: Soft to moderate (2–8 dGH)
  • Difficulty: Intermediate to advanced — solid color shows stress and poor water fastest

Overview

Cobalt is a solid-color discus strain developed in the 1970s and 80s — deep, metallic blue with noticeably less red bleed-through than Red Turquoise, but, unlike Blue Diamond, still showing visible transverse (vertical) banding across the body. That retained banding is the single clearest way to tell the two strains apart at a glance, and it's not a minor detail: it's the exact trait breeders spent another generation or more of selective work removing to get from Cobalt to Blue Diamond in the first place.

Like every farm strain on this site, Cobalt descends from the wild Symphysodon blue-complex stock, pushed through selective breeding toward a solid, saturated result instead of the wild fish's banded camouflage. What makes Cobalt worth its own page rather than a footnote on the Blue Diamond guide is that it's commercially active and genuinely sold as its own strain today — not a historical curiosity — while also being the fish Blue Diamond is bred from, which creates real confusion for buyers trying to tell the two apart.

Cobalt vs. Blue Diamond — Related, Not Identical

Long-running discussion among hobbyist breeders (notably on the SimplyDiscus forum, where posters with decades in the strain trade have argued over the exact history) disagrees on several details of Blue Diamond's origin, but converges on one point: Blue Diamond was developed from Cobalt-line stock, not as an unrelated project. Several accounts credit Hong Kong-area breeders of the late 1970s and early 1980s with crossing large, near-solid blue Cobalt fish against "Ghost" discus (a pattern-suppressing line) to strip out the last visible striping; other accounts place the key breeding work with operations in Penang and Kuala Lumpur in the early 1990s, with Lo Wing Yat of World Wide Fish Farms most often credited — though even that credit is disputed by other posters in the same discussions, some of whom argue he showed and improved the strain rather than originating it. No single account should be treated as settled fact, but every version agrees Cobalt is the starting material the Blue Diamond result was bred out of, which is the part that actually matters for a buyer.

The practical upshot: a true Blue Diamond should show no transverse banding at all in the face or fins. Cobalt is allowed to keep faint-to-moderate banding, and often shows it more clearly when the fish is resting or mildly stressed. A solid blue fish with any visible barring that's being sold as "Blue Diamond" is more honestly described as a Cobalt, or a Cobalt/Blue Diamond cross partway toward the fully pattern-free result — see our Blue Diamond guide for what a true specimen of that strain should look like by comparison.

Tank Setup & Water

Baseline requirements track every other farm strain on this site — see the Red Turquoise guide for the full breakdown of tank sizing, hardscape, and filtration. A few notes specific to Cobalt:

  • Tank: 55 gallons minimum for a group of four to six juveniles growing out, moving toward 75–125 gallons as adults reach 6–8 inches. Height matters as much as footprint — 20 inches or taller.
  • Substrate: Bare-bottom is common among keepers focused on breeding or showing, for the same water-quality-discipline reasons covered on the Blue Diamond page.
  • Water chemistry: Standard discus range — pH 6.0–7.2, 82–86°F, 2–8 dGH.
  • Lighting & background: Moderate, even lighting and a dark background show off the metallic blue more clearly than bright or heavily blue-spectrum lighting, the same display logic that applies to Blue Diamond.

Feeding

Standard discus feeding — a quality discus pellet as the staple, rotated with beef heart mix and frozen bloodworms, per the feeding guide. As with Blue Diamond, carotenoid- and spirulina-inclusive foods are commonly credited by breeders with helping intensify blue tones, though genetics and consistently clean water do most of the actual work.

Temperament & Compatibility

No different from other discus strains — peaceful, shoaling, happiest in groups of six or more, and fully compatible with the Amazon-biotope tankmates covered in the Red Turquoise guide (cardinal tetras, corydoras, rummynose tetras, otocinclus). Mixing Cobalt with patterned strains in one display tank causes no behavioral issues.

Breeding Notes

Standard discus pairing and spawning applies — see the breeding guide for pair formation, conditioning, spawning, and the parental slime-feeding stage. Cobalt remains an active working strain for breeders aiming at Blue Diamond: a Cobalt pair can throw a mix of offspring ranging from heavily banded to nearly pattern-free, and breeders selecting toward true Blue Diamond cull for the least-banded fry across multiple generations rather than expecting any one pairing to produce a finished result. A home pairing between two attractive Cobalt adults won't reliably reproduce either a consistent Cobalt look or Blue Diamond-grade pattern-free offspring — this is multi-generation, dedicated-breeder territory, same as it is for Blue Diamond.

Common Issues

Mistaken for (or Mislabeled as) Blue Diamond

Cause: The two strains are closely related and visually similar at a glance, and some sellers use "Blue Diamond" loosely for any near-solid blue fish.

Fix: Not a health issue — a buying-information one. Check the face and fins closely for any transverse banding; its presence means you're looking at Cobalt (or a Cobalt-leaning cross), not a true Blue Diamond, and that should be reflected in price and expectations.

Fading or Patchy Color

Cause: Almost always a water-quality or stress issue rather than illness — a missed water change, a disrupted group hierarchy, or a chemistry swing, the same pattern covered on the Blue Diamond page.

Fix: Test water immediately and check group dynamics. Color should recover within days once the stressor is resolved; persistent fading despite good water is worth a closer look for underlying illness — see common diseases.

Where to Get Quality Fish

Look for deep, even metallic-blue saturation and clean fins; some visible transverse banding is expected and normal for genuine Cobalt, so don't mistake it for a flaw the way you would on a fish sold as Blue Diamond. Pet Zone SD stocks Cobalt and Blue Diamond stock when available, sourced and acclimated rather than rushed to the sales floor — ask which strain, specifically, a given fish is if the distinction matters to your project. See our sourcing guide for general buying advice.

Cost: Typically in the same $20–40 range as other solid-blue strains for quality juveniles, with exceptionally even, heavily-saturated specimens commanding a premium.

Final Thoughts

Cobalt is a satisfying solid-color strain in its own right, not just a stepping stone to something else — but understanding its direct relationship to Blue Diamond makes you a sharper buyer for either fish. If banding-free is the specific look you're after, hold out for a true Blue Diamond; if you like the deep metallic blue with a bit of the wild-type pattern still showing through, Cobalt is the real strain to ask for by name.

Cobalt Discus FAQ

What's the difference between Cobalt and Blue Diamond discus?

Visible banding. A true Blue Diamond shows no transverse bars at all in the face or fins, while Cobalt keeps faint-to-moderate vertical banding visible, especially under stress or at rest. Genetically and historically the two are closely related rather than unrelated strains — most breeder accounts treat Cobalt as the starting stock that Blue Diamond was developed from, not a separate project.

Is Cobalt discus the same strain as Blue Diamond under a different name?

No, though sellers sometimes blur the line. Cobalt and Blue Diamond are closely related, not identical — a true Blue Diamond is bred further toward zero visible striping, while Cobalt is allowed to keep some. A fish with any visible banding sold as "Blue Diamond" is more accurately a Cobalt or a Cobalt/Blue Diamond cross.

Where did Cobalt discus come from?

A solid metallic-blue strain developed in the 1970s-80s, predating Blue Diamond. Breeder accounts from that era disagree on exactly who first crossed Cobalt-line fish toward the striping-free Blue Diamond result, with competing credit going to breeders associated with Hong Kong, Penang, and Kuala Lumpur operations in the early 1990s — but every version of the story treats Cobalt as the starting material, not a rival strain developed independently.

Does Cobalt discus need different care than other discus?

No — standard discus parameters apply: 82-86°F, pH 6.0-7.2, soft to moderate hardness, and the same 55-gallon-minimum tall tank as any other strain. As a solid-color strain it shows stress and poor water quality a bit more visibly than a patterned strain, the same practical note that applies to Blue Diamond.
