Feeding Discus While You're Away

By Pet Zone SD Team • November 2026

The Essentials
  • Healthy adults handle roughly a week unfed; juveniles and grow-out fish don't
  • Feed heavily and do a water change right before you leave — skip the filter cleaning
  • Auto-feeders only dispense dry pellets; keepers genuinely disagree on trusting them
  • Vacation feeder blocks carry a real water-fouling risk — treat them as a last resort, not a default
  • A trained sitter beats any gadget for trips longer than about a week

Why This Is Harder With Discus Than Most Fish

A betta or a goldfish shrugging off a missed meal doesn't put its tank at risk. Discus are a different case for two reasons that compound each other: the feeding schedule this site recommends is already two to three meals a day for adults and three to four for juveniles, and that feeding happens in water held at 82–86°F, where uneaten food and waste break down fast and the water-change routine is correspondingly aggressive — 25–50% every one to two days for serious keepers. Vacation planning for discus isn't really a feeding problem in isolation; it's a bioload-management problem with feeding as the trigger. Get the feeding plan wrong while you're gone and the water-quality consequences show up faster than they would in a cooler, less heavily-fed community tank.

How Long Can Discus Actually Go Without Food?

Experienced discus keepers on long-running forums like Aquaria Central report that healthy adults handle about a week fasting without issue, provided they're fed well beforehand. The usual pre-trip routine: feed generously in the days before departure, do a water change as close to the departure date as possible, and keep the lights off (or on a shorter cycle) while you're gone — fish left in the dark are less active and burn through less reserve. That's meaningfully longer than the general, species-agnostic guidance aquatic veterinarians give for fish overall, which caps a comfortable fast at three to five days and treats ten to fourteen as an occasional stretch rather than something to plan around. The two aren't really in conflict — it's a case where discus-specific keeper experience runs more permissive than general caution — but it's worth knowing both numbers exist rather than treating either as the final word. For a trip of three days or less, the simplest and most reliable plan is still to not feed at all and skip the gadgets entirely.

Automatic Feeders: Do They Work for Discus?

This is a real, unresolved split among discus keepers, not a settled best practice. One long-running Aquaria Central thread on this exact question states flatly: "I do not trust auto feeders. Read too many horror stories when they malfunction when people are away from the house" — and recommends a sitter instead, even an inexperienced one doing nothing more than a quick headcount and a pinch of pre-measured food twice during the trip. Set against that, a Monster Fish Keepers poster running a heavily stocked 150-gallon discus tank reports using an auto-feeder as "the way to go" when nobody's home, with no issues even after going over a month without a water change or filter cleaning in that same tank (river-rock substrate, in their account, doing a lot of the biological heavy lifting). Both are real, direct accounts from people actually keeping discus — this guide isn't going to pretend one is simply right and the other wrong.

What's less disputed: a standard belt or wheel auto-feeder only dispenses dry food — crushed flake or pellets. It cannot deliver beef heart mix, frozen bloodworms, or live foods, so even a feeder that works mechanically is still a forced diet change for however long you're gone. If you do use one, aquatic-vet guidance is consistent on one point regardless of species — test it running for several days before you leave so you can see the actual portion size land in the tank, not the portion size you think you programmed.

Vacation Feeder Blocks: A Real Risk, Not Just an Inconvenience

These gel or plaster blocks are marketed as working fine for small to medium fish over three to fourteen days, and that's a fair summary of how they're generally positioned. But it's worth weighing that against a specific, named account from Jack Dempsey of TropicalFishCareGuides.com, writing for Pet Sitters International, who reports using slow-release feeding tablets and "actually" losing fish to them — his explanation being that they often don't release enough food to actually feed the tank while doing a genuinely effective job of clouding and contaminating the water instead. That's the core problem for discus specifically: a block that fails quietly doesn't just leave the fish a little hungry, it adds exactly the kind of water-quality insult discus tolerate worst, with nobody home to catch it. Discus are also larger, more food-driven cichlids than the small community fish these blocks are usually sized for, which is one more reason to treat them as a last resort for a short trip rather than a default plan.

The Water-Quality Side: What to Do Before You Leave

Do a water change in the day or two before departure — not a deep clean. Aqueon's own vacation-prep guidance (and the practice described by the discus keepers above) agrees on avoiding a major filter cleaning or substrate disturbance in that same window: you want the bacterial colony undisturbed and the nitrogen cycle running on its established rhythm while no one's watching for a spike, not freshly reset right before an unattended stretch. Don't add new fish or make other changes to the tank in the weeks before an extended trip either, for the same reason — anything that needs monitoring should happen while you're still there to monitor it.

If your trip runs long enough that you're relying on any automated feeding at all, scale the portion down from your normal routine rather than matching it. A slightly underfed tank for a week is a minor, recoverable issue; an overfed, unmonitored tank running at 82–86°F for the same week is how a vacation habit turns into a water-quality emergency.

Equipment Checks Before You Go

  • Heater: Confirm it's actually holding 82–86°F with a separate thermometer, not just trusting the heater's own dial — a failed heater is a far bigger risk to discus than a missed meal.
  • Filter: Check for free flow and clean media with plenty of lead time before you leave, never the day of departure.
  • Lighting: Put it on a timer if it isn't already, so the tank keeps a normal day/night cycle whether or not anyone's home to flip a switch.
  • Air pumps, if used: Confirm lines are secure and clear, with a check valve in place so a power blip can't siphon tank water back through the line.

When a Sitter Is the Right Call

For trips longer than about a week, or for any grow-out or breeding tank, an actual caretaker beats any gadget — this is where the two discus-keeper accounts above genuinely agree, even the one comfortable with auto-feeders as backup. Pre-portion every feeding into a pill box or labeled bag so an inexperienced sitter can't over- or under-feed by guessing, write down the normal tank temperature so they have something concrete to check against, and tell them how many fish are actually in the tank — a dead discus decomposing unnoticed for several days will spike ammonia faster than almost anything else that could go wrong while you're gone. Leave your phone number along with a backup contact (a local aquarium store or an experienced fishkeeping friend) in case something comes up that the written instructions don't cover.

Juveniles & Grow-Out Tanks Need a Different Plan

Everything above assumes a mature display group. It doesn't transfer to juveniles. General aquatic-vet guidance caps comfortable fasting for juvenile fish at roughly 12 to 24 hours, and this site's own feeding guide already treats underfeeding at this life stage as a leading, largely irreversible cause of stunting — not a minor setback that catches back up later. A grow-out tank left for anything beyond an overnight trip needs a real caretaker feeding several times a day, or at minimum a tested auto-feeder running a schedule you've already verified works, not the week-long fasting tolerance that's reasonable for settled adults.

Vacation Care FAQ

How long can discus go without being fed?

Healthy, well-conditioned adults can comfortably handle about a week unfed, according to experienced discus keepers, if they're fed heavily beforehand and the tank gets a water change right before you leave. General veterinary guidance for aquarium fish overall is more conservative — three to five days as the comfortable ceiling, up to ten to fourteen as a stretch, not a target. For a trip of three days or less, skip feeding entirely rather than risk a feeder malfunction.

Do automatic fish feeders work for discus?

Opinions among discus keepers genuinely split. Standard belt/wheel feeders handle dry pellets reasonably well, and several keepers run heavily stocked discus tanks on them successfully for weeks. Others flatly don't trust them, citing malfunctions that dump an entire hopper at once or jam and dispense nothing. Either way, a feeder only dispenses dry pellets — it can't deliver beef heart, frozen, or live food, so a trip longer than a few days still means a real diet change for the fish.

Are vacation feeder blocks safe for discus?

Treat them with real caution. They're generally marketed as fine for small to medium fish, but at least one experienced source reports losing fish to them directly, because they can under-release and foul the water with cloudy residue rather than reliably feeding. Given discus's sensitivity to water quality, a malfunctioning block is a worse outcome than a few unfed days.

Should I feed my discus extra before going on vacation?

Yes — feed generously in the days before you leave, then do a water change as close to departure as possible so the tank starts the trip clean. Avoid cleaning the filter or disturbing substrate in that same pre-trip window; you want the bacterial colony left alone, not freshly disrupted, right before nobody's home to watch it.

Can juvenile or grow-out discus skip meals like adults can?

No. General guidance for aquarium fish caps comfortable fasting for juveniles at roughly 12 to 24 hours, and this site's own feeding guide already flags underfeeding as a leading, largely irreversible cause of stunting at this life stage. A grow-out tank needs an actual caretaker or a tested feeder for anything beyond an overnight trip — don't apply the adult week-long fasting tolerance to young fish.
