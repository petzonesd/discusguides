Tank Setup & Equipment

By Pet Zone SD Team • September 2026

The Essentials
  • Minimum: 55 gallons for a group of 4–6 juveniles, 75–125 for adults
  • Height over footprint — 20"+ tall tanks, discus are deep-bodied
  • Bare-bottom for breeding/grow-out; planted for display
  • Gentle to moderate flow — canister or sponge filtration
  • A reliable, accurate heater is not optional equipment

Tank Size & Footprint

The floor is 55 gallons for a small group of four to six juveniles growing out, with an eye toward upgrading as they mature. A group of six adults reaching 6–8 inches genuinely wants 75–125 gallons of long-term space. Height matters as much as footprint — discus are tall, laterally-compressed, disc-shaped fish, and a tank under 20 inches tall visibly cramps them. This is the opposite priority from most cichlids, where floor space rules; with discus, buy the tall tank over the long one.

Discus are also a shoaling fish outside of an established breeding pair, not a solo centerpiece. A lone discus, or a mismatched pair without a buffer group, tends to sit dark and stressed in a corner. Six is a workable minimum for a display tank — more is better up to what your gallons and filtration can support.

Substrate: The Real Debate

Serious discus breeders overwhelmingly run bare-bottom tanks — no substrate at all, sometimes not even a background — because it makes daily siphoning of waste and uneaten food trivial, and waste control is the single biggest lever on discus health. If your priority is breeding, growing out juveniles, or a quarantine/hospital setup, bare-bottom is the practical, low-argument choice.

For a display tank, a heavily planted or hardscaped Amazon-biotope setup with sand or fine gravel is entirely workable and arguably more true to how discus actually live in the wild — provided you're disciplined about gravel-vac maintenance and don't let organic waste accumulate down in the substrate where it's harder to remove. This is a genuine trade-off, not a right-and-wrong argument: bare-bottom optimizes for water quality and ease of maintenance, planted optimizes for display and behavioral naturalism. Pick based on which one you'll actually keep up with long-term.

Hardscape & Cover

Driftwood, broad-leafed plants (Amazon sword, large Anubias, Java fern), and open swimming lanes suit the fish's natural habitat and give shy or subordinate individuals somewhere to retreat without disappearing entirely — you still want a clear sightline to the group. Avoid anything sharp-edged or rough; discus have a delicate, easily-scraped body and a mucus coat that's part of how they feed their fry.

Breeding cones or vertical slate: If there's any chance of a pair forming, include at least one broad, smooth, near-vertical surface — a purpose-made ceramic breeding cone, a piece of slate on edge, or a large sword-plant leaf will all work. Discus lay eggs on vertical surfaces, not in caves, a meaningful behavioral difference from most other popular cichlids that shapes how you should furnish the tank from day one.

Filtration & Flow

Discus prefer gentle to moderate flow — strong current stresses a fish shaped like a dinner plate and works against its swimming style. Canister filters and sponge filters both work well; filter capacity matters more than flow rate, because filtration in a discus tank is really about keeping ahead of a heavily-fed, warm-water tank's bioload rather than about turnover speed.

  • Canister filters: The standard choice for display tanks — high media capacity, adjustable flow, easy to tuck out of sight.
  • Sponge filters: Popular for breeding and grow-out tanks — gentle flow, safe for fry, and simple to run in multiples across several bare-bottom tanks.
  • Whatever you choose, oversize it. Discus tanks run a heavier bioload than the fish-count alone suggests, because of how much and how often they're fed.

Remember that the filter is not the real workhorse here — water changes are. No filter, however large, replaces the routine covered in full in the water chemistry guide.

Heating

82–86°F is well above the range most tropical community fish tolerate long-term, which is one reason discus get their own species tank. A quality heater with a reliable, accurate thermostat is essential equipment, not a nice-to-have — heater failure in either direction (a stuck-on heater cooking the tank, or a dead heater letting it crash) can wipe out a group fast in a tank running this warm. In anything over 40 gallons, consider two smaller heaters rather than one large one, both for even heat distribution and for redundancy if one fails.

Lighting

Discus don't have strong lighting requirements themselves, but wild-type and heirloom strains in particular tend to show their best color under moderate, warm-toned lighting rather than bright, blue-heavy reef-style LEDs, which can wash out subtle patterning. If the tank is planted, size the lighting to the plants' needs and let the discus benefit as a side effect — dimmer, cover-rich tanks also tend to produce more relaxed, less flighty fish.

Quarantine Setup

A dedicated quarantine tank — even a modest bare-bottom 20–29 gallon with a sponge filter and heater — is close to mandatory for anyone buying discus regularly. Quarantine every new arrival for two to four weeks before it ever sees the main tank; this single habit prevents the majority of disease introductions covered in the common diseases guide.

Tank Setup FAQ

What size tank do discus need?

55 gallons minimum for a small group of 4-6 juveniles growing out, and 75-125 gallons for a group of adults long-term. Height matters as much as footprint — look for tanks at least 20 inches tall, since discus are deep-bodied fish.

Should a discus tank be bare-bottom or planted?

Bare-bottom is standard for breeding, grow-out, and quarantine tanks because it makes waste removal trivial. A planted or hardscaped display tank works too, provided you stay disciplined about gravel-vac maintenance — it's a trade-off between ease of maintenance and display naturalism, not a right-or-wrong choice.

Do discus need a strong filter?

They need filter capacity, not strong flow — discus prefer gentle to moderate current. Canister filters suit display tanks; sponge filters suit breeding and grow-out setups. Oversize whatever you choose, since discus tanks carry a heavier bioload than the fish count alone suggests.

Do I need a quarantine tank for discus?

Yes — a modest bare-bottom 20-29 gallon quarantine tank with a sponge filter and heater is close to mandatory. Quarantining every new arrival for two to four weeks before it joins the main tank prevents most disease introductions covered in common diseases.
