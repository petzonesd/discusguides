Water Chemistry Mastery

By Pet Zone SD Team • September 2026

The Essentials
  • pH: 6.0–7.4 (farm-raised stock is flexible; breeding favors 6.0–6.8)
  • Temperature: 82–86°F (28–30°C)
  • Hardness: 2–10 dGH, soft to moderate
  • Ammonia / Nitrite: 0 ppm, no exceptions, ever
  • Nitrate: under 20 ppm, ideally under 10 ppm
  • Water changes: 25–50% every 1–2 days for serious keepers

Why Water Chemistry Is Non-Negotiable With Discus

More discus die from water quality than from disease, and most of what looks like disease — hole-in-the-head, chronic dark coloring, stunted growth, "discus plague" — traces back to water that was allowed to drift. Discus evolved in the slow, structurally complex, tannin-stained tributaries of the Amazon basin, where ammonia and nitrite are functionally zero and organic waste doesn't accumulate the way it does in a closed aquarium. Your job as a keeper is to recreate that stability artificially, and the tool for that is a testing and water-change routine you actually stick to, not a one-time setup you configure and forget.

The good news is that discus chemistry isn't complicated to understand. It's demanding to maintain. There's a real difference between those two things, and most discus failures come from underestimating the maintenance half.

The Core Parameters

pH

ContextTarget
General keeping (farm-raised stock)6.0–7.4
Optimal display range6.2–6.8
Active breeding6.0–6.8, softer water improves fertility

Modern Asian farm lines are bred on neutral-to-slightly-hard tap water and tolerate a wider pH band than their wild ancestors — this is genuinely good news for hobbyists without an RO/DI setup. That said, don't chase a specific pH number by dumping chemical pH adjusters into the tank. Swings are far more dangerous than a stable pH that's merely "not perfect." If your tap water sits at a steady 7.2 and your fish are eating, coloring up, and behaving normally, leave it alone.

Temperature

82–86°F is the range that surprises new keepers most, since it sits well above what most tropical community fish tolerate long-term — this is the single biggest reason discus get a species-only tank rather than joining a general community setup. Invest in a reliable, accurate heater (or two smaller heaters for redundancy in a large tank) with a separate thermometer to verify it — heater failure in either direction is a fast way to lose an entire group. Higher temperatures within the range also modestly boost metabolism and immune response, which is part of why raising temperature is a standard first response to early illness.

Hardness

2–10 dGH covers most successful discus keeping. Wild-caught fish and serious breeding programs push toward the softer end (2–4 dGH) to mimic blackwater conditions and improve egg fertilization rates; farm-raised community stock is considerably more forgiving. If your tap water runs hard (12+ dGH), most discus will still survive and often thrive, but breeding success drops and you'll want to blend in RO water for a spawning pair.

Ammonia and Nitrite

Zero, always, no exception. Discus have almost no tolerance for either compound — even 0.25 ppm sustained for a few days is enough to damage gills and open the door to secondary infection. This is why a fully cycled tank before fish go in, and weekly ammonia/nitrite testing during the first two months of a new tank's life, is not optional. A cycled tank with a heavy bioload (discus eat a lot and produce a lot of waste) can still spike if filtration falls behind — test, don't assume.

Nitrate

Keep it under 20 ppm, and under 10 ppm if you're breeding or growing out juveniles. Nitrate is the slow, cumulative parameter that water changes exist to control — it won't kill a discus in a day the way ammonia can, but chronically elevated nitrate is strongly linked to stalled growth, dulled color, and increased disease susceptibility over weeks and months.

The Water Change Routine That Actually Matters

This is the single most repeated piece of advice in the discus hobby, and it's repeated because it's the real lever. Serious keepers change 25–50% of tank volume every one to two days, especially with juveniles, a breeding pair, or a heavily-stocked grow-out tank. A mature, lightly-stocked display tank can sometimes stretch to every-other-day or twice-weekly large changes, but daily-to-near-daily is the standard for anyone serious about growth rate, color development, or breeding.

This is a materially heavier commitment than almost any other freshwater fish, and it's the main reason discus have a reputation for being demanding — not because the fish are fragile, but because the maintenance bar is genuinely higher. Skipping water changes is the single most common root cause behind hole-in-the-head disease, stunted growth, and dark, listless fish that "just aren't thriving" for no obvious reason.

Make it sustainable

A python-style gravel vacuum/hose system that connects directly to a sink cuts a 50% water change from a 45-minute chore to a 10-minute one. If your routine depends on hauling buckets, you will eventually skip changes when life gets busy — invest in the hose system early, not after you've already had a health scare.

Testing: What to Use and How Often

  • Liquid test kits (API Freshwater Master Test Kit or equivalent) are meaningfully more accurate than test strips for ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate — worth the extra minute per test.
  • New tank (first 6–8 weeks): Test ammonia and nitrite daily until you're confident the biological filter is fully established.
  • Established tank: Weekly full-panel testing (pH, ammonia, nitrite, nitrate) is a reasonable baseline; step it up to every few days if you notice any behavior change.
  • Always test after any change — a new filter, a substrate change, a bout of illness, or a period of heavier feeding.

Dealing With Difficult Tap Water

Hard, Alkaline Tap Water

Blend RO (reverse osmosis) water with tap water to bring hardness and pH down for breeding setups. For general keeping with farm-raised stock, many keepers run straight tap water successfully — don't over-engineer a display tank if your fish are already doing well.

Soft, Acidic Tap Water

Good news for discus, but watch pH stability — very soft water has little buffering capacity and can swing further than you'd expect. Crushed coral or a small buffer product can stabilize pH without pushing it into hard-water territory.

Chlorine and Chloramine

Always treat incoming tap water with a dechlorinator rated for chloramine, not just chlorine — most municipal supplies use chloramine now, and discus are notably sensitive to it. Match the water change and treatment closely in time; don't let treated water sit around unmixed for long periods before it goes in the tank.

Water Chemistry FAQ

What's the ideal pH for discus?

6.0–7.4 covers most successful keeping with modern farm-raised stock, with 6.2–6.8 as the optimal display range and 6.0–6.8 favored for active breeding. Stability matters more than hitting an exact number — a steady 7.2 beats a pH you're constantly chasing.

Can discus live in tap water?

Often yes. Modern Asian farm lines are bred on neutral-to-slightly-hard tap water and tolerate a wider pH and hardness band than their wild ancestors. If your tap water is stable and your fish are eating and coloring up normally, there's no need to over-engineer it with RO water.

Do discus need an RO (reverse osmosis) system?

Not for general keeping with farm-raised stock. RO water becomes genuinely useful for active breeding programs or if your tap water runs very hard (12+ dGH) and you want to improve egg fertilization rates.

Why is ammonia so dangerous for discus?

Discus evolved in Amazon waters where ammonia and nitrite run functionally at zero year-round, so they have almost no tolerance for either compound — even 0.25 ppm sustained for a few days can damage gills and open the door to infection. Ammonia and nitrite must read zero, always.
